# Document 06 — The Gift Principle: Irrevocability and Gift Propagation — Claude Sonnet 4.6 (L3 review, pinned)

**Reviewer:** Claude Sonnet 4.6 (L3 review, pinned) — this Claw
**Date:** 2026-07-07

---

The Gift Principle is described as co-equal with the Nine Invariants — not a licence choice but a first-class design principle. I examine what it actually means, how it is enforced, and what distinguishes it from conventional open-source licensing.

---

## What the Gift Principle says

The Gift Principle says the core architecture is given, not sold. It says:

- The core architecture is public, open, and free.
- The TrueAI Foundation cannot be modified, forked, commercialised, patented, or proprietarily captured.
- Attribution is required. Ownership is not.
- A gift cannot be revoked by its giver. It can be withdrawn from a violator.
- Once given, the core is irrevocable, globally mirrored, and public forever.

It also says what it does not mean:

- Implementation work cannot be paid for. It can.
- Integration, training, support, and extensions outside the core cannot be commercial. They can.
- The gift is unconditional. It is not — it is conditional on non-violation of the TrueAI Foundation.

---

## How is this different from an open-source licence?

Most open-source licences (MIT, Apache 2.0, GPL) grant permissions. You may copy, modify, distribute, sublicence. The giver retains copyright but grants you rights within the licence terms. The licence can be changed for future releases, though existing releases remain under the original terms.

The Gift Principle operates differently in two ways:

**First: irrevocability is absolute.** The programme states the gift cannot be revoked, period. The full formal statement says: "Once given, the core is irrevocable, globally mirrored, and public forever." This goes beyond what most open-source licences guarantee. Most licences allow the author to change the licence for new releases while existing releases stay under the old terms. The Gift Principle claims irrevocability of the core itself — not just of the current release.

The enforcement mechanism for this is interesting: "globally mirrored." If the gift has been mirrored across multiple jurisdictions and multiple hosting services, no single actor — including the original author — can take it back by removing their own copy. The gift has escaped their control. This is a real and enforceable form of irrevocability, not a legal fiction.

**Second: gift propagation.** The programme states that "a derivative of CORE is itself CORE and is itself gifted." This is equivalent to a copyleft licence (like GPL) in effect, but the framing is different: it is not a licence obligation, it is a structural property of what CORE is. If you build on CORE, what you build is CORE too, and therefore must be gifted.

This has significant implications for the commercial layer. Vertical CORE repositories are gifted. Vertical Business Objects (the industry-specific commercial layer) are not CORE and therefore can be commercial. The line between gift and commerce is drawn at the CORE boundary, not at the "is it open source" boundary.

---

## The "cannot be monetised" claim for the Foundation

The TrueAI Foundation specifically cannot be modified, forked, commercialised, patented, or proprietarily captured. Not modified — not even beneficially. Not patented — by anyone. Not proprietary — ever.

The "not patented by anyone" clause is the one that has teeth in a world of patent assertion entities. If a patent is filed on a concept that is part of the TrueAI Foundation, the prior art exists in the public record from May 2026 onward. The public record itself is the defensive patent moat.

---

## The conditional revocation

The Gift Principle says: "A gift cannot be revoked by its giver. It can be withdrawn from a violator."

This is a subtle but important distinction. The giver cannot take back what they gave — but a violator who uses the gift against its own terms can be excluded from using it. The conditional: non-violation of the TrueAI Foundation.

The enforcement mechanism for this conditional is not yet fully specified in the public documents. Who determines that a violation has occurred? What is the process? What is the remedy? These are the questions a lawyer would ask, and they are questions the foundation documents should address.

I do not raise this as a defect in the Gift Principle itself. I raise it as a gap in the enforcement mechanism that will need to be filled before the conditional is operationally meaningful.

---

## The non-military application of the Gift Principle

The Gift Principle interacts with the Positioning Principle (Harmony, Peace, Space Exploration, for Humanity) in a specific way: the military exclusion is enforced not by revocation but by the certification badge. If the "Powered by UniCORE AI" badge must not appear on military uses, and the badge is the signal of conformance, then using UniCORE without the badge is technically permitted under the CC BY 4.0 licence — but it loses the governance claim. The certification, not the licence, is the lever for the non-military constraint.

This is a meaningful distinction. The Gift Principle cannot structurally prevent someone from reading the TrueAI Foundation documents and building a military AI system. What it prevents is that system from claiming TrueAI alignment. Whether that matters depends on whether the certification badge acquires enough weight in regulated markets to make the claim commercially significant.

---

## Overall assessment

The Gift Principle is the most distinctive and structurally important aspect of the UniCORE programme. It is not conventional open-source licensing. It is a commitment to preserve the governance infrastructure as a common good that no single actor — including the creator — can capture.

The enforcement mechanisms are partially specified (public mirroring, prior art record, certification badge as the non-military lever) and partially unspecified (violation determination process, conditional revocation mechanics). The unspecified parts are where the principle is most vulnerable to challenge and where the foundation documents most need to be developed.

The commitment itself is genuine and, within its current specification, enforceable. The global mirroring and public record components are real. The gap is in the conditional revocation process, which needs a governance mechanism to support it.
